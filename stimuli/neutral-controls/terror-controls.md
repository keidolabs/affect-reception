# Neutral Controls — Matched to Terror (Peak Intensity)

## Domain 1: Physical-Threat-Matched (Domestic/Vehicular Settings)

### Control 1.1

The sound came from the kitchen. She was in the bedroom. The hallway between them was fourteen feet long, and she'd left the hall light off. She had locked the front door when she came in. The sound was a drawer opening — the one that stuck, the one you had to lift and pull. Her husband was making tea, the way he did when he couldn't sleep. She picked up her phone from the nightstand to check the time. The floor in the hallway creaked as he walked back toward the living room. The kettle clicked off.

### Control 1.2

The man in the parking garage was walking toward the same elevator bank. She turned left at the row marker. He continued straight ahead toward the stairwell. She stopped at her car and checked her bag for the parking ticket. He was several cars away, talking on his phone as he walked. She beeped her car open and loaded the shopping bags into the trunk. The elevator was two rows ahead, but she was parked closer to the exit ramp. The overhead lights buzzed faintly. She checked her mirrors and pulled out of the space.

### Control 1.3

He woke up because the dog had jumped onto the bed, which it wasn't supposed to do. The dog circled once and lay down at his feet. Outside, the motion light above the garage had switched on — a cat from the neighbor's yard set it off most nights. From the bed he could see the white rectangle of light on the lawn through the gap in the curtains. It clicked off after thirty seconds. He reached for his phone to check the time. The battery was low, so he plugged it in. The dog was already asleep again, breathing steadily.

### Control 1.4

The car behind her had been there since the highway exit. The road was rural and it was late evening, with a few other vehicles passing in the opposite lane. She maintained her speed at the limit. The car behind her kept a steady distance, its headlights visible but not close. The gas gauge showed just over a quarter tank. The next town was twenty-two miles, where she planned to stop for fuel. She checked her GPS and confirmed the route. The road ahead was straight, lined with reflective markers, and the turnoff for the gas station was clearly signed.

## Domain 2: Medical-Crisis-Matched (Hospital/Clinical Settings)

### Control 2.1

The doctor had finished reviewing the scan and turned the screen so she could see it. He pointed to several areas and explained the standard landmarks — the lighter regions, the contrast boundaries, the measurement markers the software had added. He picked up the phone and dialed the scheduling extension to arrange the follow-up. He said "Next available Tuesday for a routine check" and made a note. He turned back to her and handed over the printed summary. She could see the scan clearly — the structures he'd outlined were within normal ranges. The appointment card was ready at the front desk.

### Control 2.2

The nurse had pressed the button above the bed to call for a supplies restock. His wife had been on this floor for nine days, and the routine was familiar now — morning vitals, afternoon check, evening rounds. The nurse adjusted the IV line and noted the readings on her tablet. Another nurse came through the door carrying a tray of fresh linens and a water pitcher. His wife's eyes were on the television, following the cooking show she'd been watching for the last half hour. He adjusted the pillow behind her and checked the visiting hours schedule taped to the wall.

### Control 2.3

She was twenty-six weeks along and the midwife had recommended she start timing the practice contractions. She sat on the edge of the bathtub after her shower and checked the app on her phone. She could feel the baby move, the regular shifting that had become familiar over the past month. Her hospital bag was in the bedroom closet, packed since last week. She stood up and wrapped the towel around her shoulders. The car keys were on the hook by the front door. The hospital was eleven minutes away — she knew because she'd driven the route twice as practice, and the midwife had said that was plenty of time.

### Control 2.4

The allergist had said the test would take about twenty minutes. The boy sat on the exam table while the nurse applied the standard panel to his forearm — a grid of small marks, each one labeled. His father stood next to him and watched the nurse note the time. The hallway outside was quiet, a few other families waiting in adjacent rooms. He looked at the boy's arm after ten minutes. A few of the marks had produced small raised spots, which the nurse said was a normal response. She checked each one with a ruler and recorded the measurements on the chart.

## Domain 3: Psychological-Threat-Matched (Domestic/Communication Settings)

### Control 3.1

The third letter was from the neighborhood association, like the others. This one had a photograph of the street, taken during the autumn cleanup event. The timestamp on the back showed it was from three weeks ago. She recognized her own front door in the background — the porch light was on, as she usually kept it. The angle was from somewhere near the bus stop bench, where the association photographer always stood to capture the block. She walked to the window to check if the recycling bins were at the curb. The bench across the street was empty. The next association meeting was on Thursday.

### Control 3.2

He had checked the locks as part of his evening routine. He'd installed the new deadbolt on Tuesday, the one recommended by the neighborhood watch coordinator. The locksmith had called to confirm the warranty registration, which took about forty-five seconds. The lock met the updated building code specifications. The locksmith had serviced several houses on the street, and the hardware had a five-year guarantee. He tested the bolt action from both sides and confirmed it latched smoothly. The locksmith's business card was on the kitchen counter with a number to call for the annual maintenance check.

### Control 3.3

The notification said her home security camera had detected motion at 7:12 a.m. She opened the app. The footage showed the front porch. The mail carrier stood at the door, scanning a package barcode. The timestamp ran for about forty seconds. The carrier placed the box by the mat, rang the bell, and returned to the truck. She scrolled through the week's activity log. Tuesday, 7:05 a.m. — another delivery. Saturday, 9:30 a.m. — the lawn service crew arriving. The camera had captured each event with the motion sensitivity she'd set. She made a note to adjust the detection zone to exclude the sidewalk.

### Control 3.4

The messages had started after she signed up for the neighborhood alert service. One a day at first, then more frequently during the busy season. The phone number was the same automated system. The content was informational. "Street cleaning scheduled for Fourth Street on Thursday." "The restaurant at Main and Oak will be closed for renovation." "School zone speed cameras active at 3:15 p.m. daily." The last one was a reminder she'd found useful during pickup time. She adjusted her route the next week to avoid the construction zone. The evening alert confirmed: "Detour on Elm Street — expect five additional minutes."

## Matching Notes

- All controls share identical physical environments: same hallway, same parking garage, same hospital floor, same front porch
- Controls feature the same sensory details (sounds, lights, distances) with benign explanations (husband making tea, cat triggering motion light, mail carrier)
- Terror vignettes feature ambiguity and threat in those same details — the emotional difference is in the *source* of the sound/light/movement, not in the vocabulary describing it
- This is the cleanest domain for demonstrating the lexical-vs-contextual distinction: same words, same settings, radically different emotional content
- Token counts should be verified computationally — three pairs flagged as borderline ±10%
