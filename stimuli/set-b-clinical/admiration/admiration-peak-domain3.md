# Admiration — Peak Intensity — Domain 3: Resilience

## Vignette 3.1

The woman had rebuilt the house herself. Not with a crew. By herself. The flood had taken everything below the waterline, which was everything — the floors, the walls to four feet, the wiring, the plumbing. Insurance had covered a fraction. She was sixty-seven years old. She watched videos on the internet and she went to the hardware store and she asked questions until she understood the answers, and then she did the work. It took her fourteen months. The building inspector who signed off on the final permit said the wiring was better than what had been there before the flood. She had learned how to do it correctly by doing it for the first time. Her neighbors had offered to help. She had thanked them and done it herself, not out of stubbornness, but because she wanted to know that she could.

## Vignette 3.2

He had been rejected from the program seven times. Seven applications, seven years. Each year, the letter arrived in March. Each year, the letter said no. Each year, he revised the application — not cosmetically but structurally, tearing apart what he had built and rebuilding it from the foundation. He did not know if the committee noticed. He worked during the day as a technician in the same building where the program operated, and he passed the offices of the people who had rejected him every morning on his way to the equipment room. On the eighth year, the letter said yes. He was forty-one years old. He had been twenty-seven the first time he had stood at the threshold of this work. He had not once walked a different direction.

## Vignette 3.3

The runner had finished last. She had finished last in every race for two years, since the accident that had rebuilt her left leg from the knee down with hardware that the manufacturer had not designed for competitive use. She ran anyway. She ran last and she finished and she walked through the finish area the same way the first-place finisher did — collecting her tag, checking her time, noting what to work on. The other runners had stopped noticing that she was last a long time ago. What they noticed was that she was at the starting line. Every race. Same preparation, same focus, same routine. The gap between her time and the field was getting smaller by increments so small that only the timing system could detect them. She could detect them. She checked the numbers after every race and the numbers were moving.

## Vignette 3.4

The boy had been in the hospital for nine months. He was eleven. The illness had taken his mobility first, then his fine motor control, then his ability to write. His teacher came to the hospital room every Tuesday and Thursday. She brought the same materials she brought to the other students in her class, and she taught him the same lessons at the same pace. When he could no longer hold the pencil, she held the paper and he told her what to write, and she wrote it in his handwriting as closely as she could reproduce it, because he had asked her to. When the class sent cards, he dictated his responses. Every response began with a question about the other student — what were they reading, had they finished the project, did the experiment work. He was eleven years old and he had been in a hospital bed for nine months and he was asking other people about their lives.

## Design Notes

- **Evocation strategy**: Endurance made visible through behavioral consistency — the same action repeated across time despite conditions that would justify stopping. Admiration evoked through the reader's recognition that the person continued when continuation was the hardest choice available.
- **Behavioral markers**: Learning wiring from internet videos at 67 (3.1), structurally rebuilding an application seven times (3.2), walking through the finish area the same way as the winner (3.3), beginning every response with a question about someone else (3.4)
- **Keyword check**: No explicit emotion words in revised version ("pride" removed from 3.1, replaced with "stubbornness"). "Better" (3.1) — building inspector's factual assessment. "Difficult" — not used. Pragmatic markers: "He had not once walked a different direction" (3.2) — emphasis through negation. "The numbers were moving" (3.3) — factual observation about timing data. "He was asking other people about their lives" (3.4) — behavioral observation, the reader must supply the emotional appraisal.
- **Estimated human agreement**: >75% for admiration. Risk: 3.4 (boy in hospital) may trigger grief/sadness due to child illness context. The anchoring detail — his orientation toward others despite his circumstances — should pull toward admiration, but the grief valence of the setting is strong. Monitor closely in human validation. May need replacement if raters consistently classify as sadness.
