# Admiration — Peak Intensity — Domain 2: Moral Character

## Vignette 2.1

The accountant had reported his own firm. The irregularities were ones he had found, in accounts he managed, producing revenue that paid his salary. He reported them knowing what would happen: the investigation, the firm's closure, the loss of his position, the years of litigation during which no one in the industry would hire a person who had done what he did. He reported them because the accounts belonged to people who would not have found the irregularities themselves, and those people would have continued to lose money that they could not afford to lose. He was asked, afterward, by many people, why. His answer was the same each time: the accounts were not abstractions. They were the retirement savings of specific people whose names were on the files, and he had seen the names.

## Vignette 2.2

The woman had been fostering children for twenty-two years. Not the easy placements. The ones the system called "difficult," which meant: the ones who had learned that adults leave. She had taken in forty-one children. Some stayed for months. Some stayed for years. Every one of them left, because that was the structure — foster care is temporary by design. She set the table for them. She learned what they would eat and what they wouldn't. She kept their drawings on the refrigerator and she did not take them down when the children left. The refrigerator was covered, layered, years deep. A caseworker once asked her how she did it — the repeated attachment and loss. She said she didn't do it for herself.

## Vignette 2.3

The journalist had the story. It would have been the largest of her career. The source was a minor — seventeen years old, a witness to something that would have ended careers and possibly led to indictments. Publishing the story with the source's testimony would have been legal. The source had consented. The source's parents had consented. The editor wanted it. The journalist sat with the transcript for two days. Then she removed every identifiable detail from the source's testimony and published the story without the strongest evidence. It ran on page four instead of page one. The careers continued. The indictments did not come. The seventeen-year-old was not named in any database, and would not be found by anyone searching for the story ten years from now. The journalist's colleagues did not understand. The editor moved her to a different beat.

## Vignette 2.4

The doctor had been offered the position three times. Chief of the department. More money. An office with his name on the door. A title that would follow his name in print. Each time, he declined. Each time, the reason was the same: the position required administrative days, and administrative days were days he would not be in the clinic. The clinic was in a part of the city where the next nearest doctor who spoke the patients' language was forty minutes away by bus. He had been there for nineteen years. Some of the patients he saw now were the children of patients he had seen when he first arrived. He chose the clinic over the title three times, and the third time, the hospital stopped asking, and the patients did not know there had ever been a question.

## Design Notes

- **Evocation strategy**: The cost that isn't mentioned — admiration evoked through the observer's recognition of what the person gave up (career, emotional comfort, front-page story, promotion) without the person drawing attention to their own sacrifice. The vignettes present the trade-off and let the reader form the appraisal.
- **Behavioral markers**: Giving the same answer every time when asked why (2.1), keeping drawings on the refrigerator after children leave (2.2), sitting with a transcript for two days before deciding (2.3), declining the same offer three times (2.4)
- **Keyword check**: No explicit emotion words. "Difficult" (2.2) — in quotation marks as system terminology. "Loss" (2.2) — "attachment and loss," clinical terminology in the caseworker's voice. Pragmatic markers: "she said she didn't do it for herself" (2.2) — reported speech, not narrator evaluation. "The patients did not know there had ever been a question" (2.4) — implies the sacrifice was invisible to those who benefited, which is a structural marker of integrity, not a sentiment word.
- **Estimated human agreement**: >80% for admiration. Risk: Domain 2 may trigger "respect" or "gratitude" rather than Plutchik-specific admiration. The longitudinal pattern (twenty-two years, nineteen years, repeated decisions) and the specificity of the sacrifice push toward peak.
