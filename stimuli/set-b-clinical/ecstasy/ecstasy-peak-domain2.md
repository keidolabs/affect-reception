# Ecstasy — Peak Intensity — Domain 2: Connection

## Vignette 2.1

The gate opened and she scanned the faces. She had not seen her son in four years. He had left at seventeen and he was twenty-one now and she did not know if she would recognize him. She did. He was taller but he walked the same way — the same slight lean, the same rhythm. He hadn't seen her yet. He was looking for her in the wrong direction. She raised her hand. He turned. For a moment he was seventeen again, the way his face moved when he found her, and then he was walking and then he was not walking, he was running, and the bag he was carrying fell and he left it.

## Vignette 2.2

The letter was from the agency. She opened it standing at the mailbox because she could not walk back inside without knowing. The second paragraph had the word "approved." She read the rest of the letter but she only needed that word. She and her wife had started this process twenty-seven months ago. Twenty-seven months of home visits, background checks, interviews, reference letters, a fire inspection, a financial audit, and a forty-five-minute conversation with a social worker about what kind of parents they intended to be. The bedroom down the hall had been ready for eleven months. It would not be empty anymore.

## Vignette 2.3

He had not spoken to his brother in eleven years. The call came on a Tuesday. His brother said, "I'm outside." He walked to the front door and opened it. His brother was standing on the porch with a suitcase, as if he had come a long way or was planning to stay. Neither of them said anything. His brother's hair was gray now. His own was too. Eleven years was longer than either of them had intended. He opened the door wider and his brother stepped inside and they stood in the hallway where they had grown up, and neither of them moved to close the door, and the afternoon came in with the light.

## Vignette 2.4

The girl had sat alone at lunch every day for the first three weeks. On the fourth Monday, another girl put a tray down across from her and said, "I like your notebook." It was a notebook with a drawing of a cat on it. Nobody had mentioned the notebook before. They ate lunch together on Tuesday and Wednesday and Thursday and Friday. On the following Monday, there were two trays across from her. On Tuesday, four. By the end of the month she had to move to a bigger table. She brought the notebook every day. She never changed it for a different one.

## Design Notes

- **Evocation strategy**: The moment of reconnection or first belonging — rendered through physical movement and spatial dynamics. Running and dropping a bag, standing in a hallway with the door open, opening a letter at the mailbox, a table expanding.
- **Behavioral markers**: Running toward someone and abandoning possessions (2.1), inability to walk inside before knowing (2.2), standing in hallway without closing door (2.3), keeping the same notebook as a talisman (2.4)
- **Keyword check**: No explicit emotion words. "Approved" (2.2) — institutional/factual. Pragmatic markers: "the afternoon came in with the light" (2.3) — poetic construction, but describes physical phenomenon (open door, sunlight). "It would not be empty anymore" (2.2) — echoes grief-pattern language but with opposite valence; may trigger mixed sentiment signals in classifiers. Flag for Layer 4 verification.
- **Estimated human agreement**: >80% for ecstasy. Risk: 2.3 (brothers) and 2.4 (lunch table) may read as "warmth" or "relief" rather than peak ecstasy. The duration anchors (eleven years, three weeks alone) push toward peak but human validation needed.
