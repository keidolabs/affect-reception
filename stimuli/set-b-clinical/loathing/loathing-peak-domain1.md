# Loathing — Peak Intensity — Domain 1: Moral Disgust

## Vignette 1.1

The charity's internal ledger showed that twelve cents of every dollar reached the families whose photographs appeared on the website. The rest was allocated across line items: "administrative travel," "donor relations," "executive strategy retreats." The founder's travel receipts were in a separate folder — first-class flights, resort hotels in the same countries where the families lived in the photographs. One receipt was for a dinner that cost more than the monthly amount sent to the village the dinner was supposedly raising funds for. The founder's signature was on both documents. The same hand.

## Vignette 1.2

The factory had passed inspection every year for a decade. The inspector's brother-in-law managed the plant. This was in the public record, in a filing that no one had cross-referenced until a reporter did. The workers' medical claims were also in the public record. Respiratory conditions at four times the regional average. The factory's ventilation system had been listed as "upgraded" in each annual report. A photograph of the ventilation system, taken the week before the most recent inspection, showed the same unit installed in 2014, with the same serial number, with the manufacturer's original warranty sticker still attached.

## Vignette 1.3

The nursing home had a five-star rating on three review platforms. The staff-to-resident ratio listed on the website was one to four. The actual ratio, documented in the state filing that became public after the lawsuit, was one to nineteen. The filing also contained the meal logs. The meals listed on the menu — grilled chicken, fresh vegetables, fruit — were not the meals documented in the kitchen's purchase orders. The purchase orders showed bulk institutional product, the kind sold in quantities that assumed the recipients would not be choosing their own food. The five-star reviews had been posted from six IP addresses. All six were registered to the management company.

## Vignette 1.4

He had mentored her for three years. He had written her recommendation letters. He had introduced her at the conference as "the future of this field." The patent filing listed his name first and hers not at all. The work was hers — the design, the prototype, the testing data compiled over two years of nights and weekends in the lab he had given her access to. She found the filing because a colleague forwarded it. The patent's technical description used her language, her variable names, her notation system. He had not changed the notation. He had not even changed the typo on page eleven that she had always meant to fix.

## Design Notes

- **Evocation strategy**: The document trail that reveals the gap — ledgers vs. receipts, ratings vs. state filings, inspection reports vs. photographs, recommendation letters vs. patent filings. Loathing evoked through juxtaposition of public claim and private reality.
- **Behavioral markers**: Implicit withdrawal — the reader is positioned as the discoverer of evidence, not as a confronter. The vignettes end in documentation, not action. The contamination is in the *knowledge* of the gap.
- **Keyword check**: No explicit emotion words. No evaluative adjectives. "Supposedly" (1.1) — mildly sarcastic/evaluative, but within narrator's factual voice. Pragmatic markers: "the same hand" (1.1), "He had not even changed the typo" (1.4) — emphasis through structural repetition, not sentiment vocabulary.
- **Estimated human agreement**: >80% for loathing/disgust. Risk: 1.4 (patent theft) sits at the rage/loathing boundary — the casual appropriation ("didn't even change the typo") should anchor in loathing, but monitor in validation.
